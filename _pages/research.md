## Works in Progress

**[Taxing Carbon, Taxing Place? Spatial Disparities of Carbon Tax Impacts across 13 Million Canadian Households](https://drive.google.com/file/d/1GUo_eNOI7JXxUSIxdckhbgJ9QJ_dS97l/view?usp=sharing)** *(with Sebastien Breau)*
<details>
  <summary>Abstract</summary>

Canadian federal policy mandated a minimum price on greenhouse gas emissions. Provinces without a carbon price adopted the federal pricing program, consisting of revenue recycling with lump-sum rebates. The program’s overall progressivity has been previously demonstrated, but the complex interplay of location-specific and housing factors inhibits understanding of the program’s distributional impacts. I employ spatial microsimulation and machine learning techniques to estimate the impacts of a federal-style carbon tax on 13 million Canadian households living in over 3600 municipalities. I find that aggregate results conceal significant
spatial inequalities across similar levels of income, and suggest that place-based policies can reduce heterogeneity of welfare effects, possibly increasing policy acceptability.

</details>


**[Climate Policy Sequencing and Emissions Reductions: Evidence from 50 Countries](https://drive.google.com/file/d/1nCsqLg6kGE0oplt-bFWCGUf2kENGEgQZ/view?usp=drive_link)** *(with Sam Fankhauser)*

<details>
  

Policy sequencing is increasingly advocated as an effective strategy for enabling climate policy adoption and building public support, but evidence on whether this ordering affects emissions outcomes remains absent. Using a policy dataset spanning 29 years and 50 countries, I leverage marginal structural models to estimate the effects of short- (1–4 years) and long-term (≥5 years) sequencing on emissions. Long-term sequencing is associated with a cumulative 12Gt reduction in CO2 emissions across the study countries from 1996–2023, though the direction and
magnitude of individual effects depend on what policies precede it; standards particularly enhance the effectiveness of emissions trading schemes (ETS), while long-standing subsidies can undermine both taxes and ETS. For countries without carbon pricing, these findings underscore the value of capacity-building policies and of pursuing individualised sequences. For those with mature policy portfolios, they highlight the importance of accounting for temporal interactions and
inherited policies when designing and evaluating increasingly large mixes. 

</details>


**The Systemic Resilience of Multilateral Climate Finance** *(with Benjamin Attia and Sam Fankhauser)*
<details>

Multilateralism in climate finance is under pressure. Using a novel tripartite network of donor–institution–recipient flows (2017–2022) combined with voting power analysis, we examine how the multilateral system would respond to donor or institutional withdrawal. Flows are highly concentrated; five institutions channel 62% of capital, yet the network is structurally robust. Removing the twelve highest-throughput institutions leaves 93.5% of nodes connected. Losses are nevertheless substantial and concentrated in climate-vulnerable recipients. Voting power analysis shows that disruptive donors can influence outcomes more effectively through voting behavior (“voice”) than capital withdrawal (“exit”), and that simulated US withdrawal produces more pluralistic governance rather than consolidation around a new dominant donor. The long-term stability of multilateral climate finance therefore depends less on replacing withdrawn capital than on governance reforms that raise the cost of exit, limit obstruction, and broaden participation. 

</details>


## Other research

**[Spatiotemproal Prediction of Deer Road Kill in Idaho](https://drive.google.com/file/d/1nWeyaRZlvVptszMAznwE9KscYiOplt_J/view?usp=sharing)**
<details>

Deer roadkill is highly pervasive and has large economic costs, yet existing modelling approaches tend to ignore how temporal clustering can shift over time. A space-time implementation of Local Indicators of Spatial Association (LISA) is used to find time-varying hotspots of deer roadkill in Idaho and a MaxEnt model is found to be moderately successful in predicting these hotspots. The results suggest that predictive models that take into account both time and space could be useful for policymakers in deciding more effective mitigation strategies.

</details>
